**Read this if** you work at a public school or district, a private school or college that takes federal student aid, a tutoring or after-school program working with a school, or an edtech, IT, or data company that a school shares student records with.

## What FERPA is

FERPA gives parents rights over their children's education records and limits when schools can share personally identifiable information from them without consent. When a student turns 18 or enters a postsecondary school at any age, the rights move to the student, an "eligible student."

It applies to schools that receive U.S. Department of Education funds: nearly every public school and district and most colleges, including private colleges whose students use federal aid. Most private K-12 schools aren't covered, though state law or contracts may apply. FERPA doesn't regulate vendors directly. It reaches them through the school, which may share records with a contractor only under the conditions below.

**Education records** are records directly related to a student and kept by the school or someone acting for it: grades, transcripts, discipline files, special education records, K-12 nurse records, emails about a student, and data in the student information system or a learning app. Not covered: a teacher's private notes, law enforcement unit records, staff employment records, college treatment records, and alumni records made after a person stopped being a student.

## The rights

Parents and eligible students can:

- **inspect and review** the records. The school must comply within 45 days of the request.
- **ask to amend** records they believe are inaccurate or misleading, and get a hearing if the school says no.
- **consent before disclosure** of personally identifiable information, unless an exception applies.
- **file a complaint** with the Department of Education's Student Privacy Policy Office.

## When schools can share without consent

- **School officials with a legitimate educational interest**, meaning people who need the record to do their job. A contractor can count if it performs a service the school would otherwise use employees for, is under the school's direct control over how it uses the records, and follows FERPA's limits on use and redisclosure.
- **Another school** where the student seeks to enroll.
- **Directory information**, such as name, address, phone, email, photo, dates of attendance, and honors, only if the school has given public notice of what it treats as directory information and a chance to opt out. Social Security numbers never qualify.
- **Health or safety emergencies**, when needed to protect the student or others.
- **Court orders and subpoenas**, usually after a reasonable effort to notify the parent or eligible student first.
- **Audits, studies for the school, accrediting organizations, financial aid**, and **parents of a tax-dependent college student**.

Schools must log most disclosures in the student's file. Disclosures to school officials, to the parent or eligible student, of directory information, or with written consent need no log.

## Vendors and edtech

To fit the school official exception, the contract should say:

- what service the vendor performs and what data it receives,
- that the school keeps control and the vendor uses the data only for the school's purpose,
- no selling, no advertising based on student data, and no redisclosure except as the school directs,
- security requirements and breach notification,
- return or deletion of data when the contract ends.

Click-through terms often fail these tests, so read them before teachers sign up a class. The Department's Privacy Technical Assistance Center publishes model terms. Apps for children under 13 also face [COPPA](20-minutes-to-coppa.md).

## New York adds annual training

New York's Education Law § 2-d and 8 NYCRR Part 121 go further for schools, districts, and BOCES in the state. They require:

- **annual data privacy and security awareness training** for officers and employees with access to personally identifiable information,
- a data protection officer and a policy aligned with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework,
- a Parents' Bill of Rights for Data Privacy and Security, posted and included in vendor contracts,
- a data privacy and security plan from each contractor that receives student data,
- reporting of breaches and unauthorized releases.

Other states have their own student privacy laws. Check yours.

## What every staff member needs to know

FERPA doesn't require training, but schools can't comply without it, and New York requires it each year.

1. **Share records only** with school officials who need them for their job, or under a listed exception.
2. **Check before you share with a parent.** Confirm identity, and look for custody orders or an eligible student. At 18 or in college, the rights are the student's.
3. **Check the directory opt-out list** before releasing directory information or posting student work, photos, or grades.
4. **Use only approved apps and secure channels**, and double-check email recipients.
5. **Lock screens and secure paper files**, especially special education and health records.
6. **Send records requests to the right office** so the 45-day deadline is met.
7. **Report a suspected breach or misdirected record right away.**

## What the school needs on paper

- The annual FERPA notice of rights and of who counts as a school official, and the directory information notice with opt-out instructions.
- The disclosure log, vendor contracts, and an incident response procedure for student data breaches.
- In New York: the data security and privacy policy, the Parents' Bill of Rights, each vendor's plan, and annual training records ([training log](../../templates/training-log.csv)).

## Penalties and enforcement

The Student Privacy Policy Office investigates complaints and aims for voluntary compliance. The ultimate penalty, loss of Department of Education funding, is very rare. A third party that improperly rediscloses records can be barred from receiving records from the school for at least five years. Individuals can't sue under FERPA itself (*Gonzaga University v. Doe*, 2002). State laws, such as New York's, add their own enforcement, including penalties for contractors.

The usual mistakes are teachers adopting free apps with no contract, releasing directory information without checking opt-outs, and vendor contracts with no deletion terms. If you're unsure about a disclosure, ask the school's counsel first.

2. Can a district share student data with a learning app vendor without parent consent?
3. Does New York require student data privacy training?
