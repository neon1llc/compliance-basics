**Read this if** you work at a public school or district, a private school or college that takes federal student aid, a tutoring or after-school program working with a school, or an edtech, IT, or data company that a school shares student records with.

## What FERPA is

The Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA) gives parents rights over their children's education records and limits when schools can share personally identifiable information from those records without consent. When a student turns 18 or enters a college or other postsecondary school at any age, the rights move to the student, who becomes an "eligible student."

FERPA applies to schools that receive funds under a U.S. Department of Education program. That covers nearly every public school and district and most colleges, including private colleges whose students use federal student aid. Most private K-12 schools take no such funds and aren't covered, though state law or contracts may still apply.

FERPA doesn't directly regulate vendors. It reaches them through the school, which may share records with a contractor only under the conditions below.

## What counts as an education record

Records directly related to a student and kept by the school or someone acting for it: grades, transcripts, discipline files, special education records, K-12 nurse records, emails about a student, and data in the student information system or a learning app. Format doesn't matter.

Not education records: a teacher's private notes kept for their own use, law enforcement unit records, staff employment records, college treatment records used only for treatment, and alumni records made after a person stopped being a student.

## The rights

Parents and eligible students can:

- **inspect and review** the education records. The school must comply within 45 days of the request.
- **ask to amend** records they believe are inaccurate or misleading, and get a hearing if the school says no.
- **consent before disclosure** of personally identifiable information, unless an exception applies.
- **file a complaint** with the Department of Education's Student Privacy Policy Office.

Each year the school must notify them of these rights and of who counts as a school official.

## When schools can share without consent

- **School officials with a legitimate educational interest**, meaning people who need the record to do their job. A contractor or vendor can count if it performs a service the school would otherwise use employees for, is under the school's direct control for how it uses and maintains the records, and follows FERPA's limits on use and redisclosure.
- **Another school** where the student seeks to enroll.
- **Directory information**, such as name, address, phone, email, photo, dates of attendance, and honors, but only if the school has given public notice of what it treats as directory information and a chance to opt out. Social Security numbers are never directory information.
- **Health or safety emergencies**, when the information is needed to protect the student or others.
- **Court orders and subpoenas**, usually after a reasonable effort to notify the parent or eligible student first.
- **Audits, studies for the school, accrediting organizations, financial aid**, and **parents of a dependent college student** (as defined by the tax code).

Schools must log most disclosures in the student's file, showing who received the information and why. Disclosures to school officials, to the parent or eligible student, of directory information, or with written consent need no log.

## Vendors and edtech

For a vendor to fit the school official exception, the contract should say:

- what service the vendor performs and what data it receives,
- that the school keeps control and the vendor uses the data only for the school's purpose,
- no selling, no advertising based on student data, and no redisclosure except as the school directs,
- security requirements and breach notification,
- and return or deletion of data when the contract ends.

Click-through terms of service often fail these tests, so read them before teachers sign up a class. The Department's Privacy Technical Assistance Center publishes model terms. Apps used with children under 13 also face [COPPA](20-minutes-to-coppa.md).

## New York adds annual training

New York's Education Law § 2-d and 8 NYCRR Part 121 go further than FERPA for schools, districts, and BOCES in the state. Among other things they require:

- **annual data privacy and security awareness training** for officers and employees with access to personally identifiable information, covering the laws that protect it and how to comply,
- a data protection officer and a data security and privacy policy aligned with the NIST Cybersecurity Framework,
- a Parents' Bill of Rights for Data Privacy and Security, posted and included in vendor contracts,
- a data privacy and security plan from each contractor that receives student data,
- and reporting of breaches and unauthorized releases.

Other states have their own student privacy laws. Check yours.

## What every staff member needs to know

FERPA itself doesn't require training, but schools can't comply without it, and New York requires it each year.

1. **Treat student records as confidential by default.** Share only with school officials who need them for their job, or under a listed exception.
2. **Check before you share with a parent.** Confirm identity, and check for custody orders or an eligible student. Once a student is 18 or in college, the rights are theirs.
3. **Check the directory information opt-out list** before releasing any of it.
4. **Use only approved apps and secure channels.** Don't sign up for apps on your own, double-check email recipients, and don't post student work, photos, or grades without checking policy and opt-outs.
5. **Lock screens and secure paper files**, especially special education and health records.
6. **Send records requests to the right office** so the 45-day deadline is met.
7. **In an emergency, share what's needed to protect someone**, and tell an administrator afterward so it's documented.
8. **Report a suspected breach or misdirected record right away.**

## What the school needs on paper

- The annual FERPA notification, the directory information notice with opt-out instructions, and a policy defining school officials.
- The record of disclosures, where required, and contracts with every vendor that receives student records.
- In New York: the data security and privacy policy, the Parents' Bill of Rights, each vendor's data privacy and security plan, and annual training records. See the [training log](../../templates/training-log.csv).
- An incident response procedure for student data breaches.

## Penalties and enforcement

The Student Privacy Policy Office investigates complaints. Its aim is voluntary compliance, and the law's ultimate penalty is the loss of Department of Education funding, which is very rare in practice. A third party that improperly rediscloses records can be barred from receiving records from the school for at least five years. Individuals can't sue under FERPA itself, as the Supreme Court held in *Gonzaga University v. Doe* (2002). State laws, such as New York's, add their own enforcement, including penalties for third-party contractors.

The usual mistakes are teachers adopting free apps with no contract, releasing directory information without checking opt-outs, sharing records with someone who has no authority, and vendor contracts with no deletion terms. If you're unsure about a disclosure, ask the school's counsel or the Student Privacy Policy Office before you release the record.
