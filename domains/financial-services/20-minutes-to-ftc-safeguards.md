**Read this if** you're a tax preparer, accountant, mortgage broker, lender, auto dealer that finances or leases, check casher, money transmitter, appraiser, debt collector, credit counselor, or other non-bank finance company, or a college in federal student aid programs. IT providers, software vendors, and bookkeepers who serve these businesses should read it too, because the rule requires clients to oversee them.

## What the rule is

The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA) requires financial institutions to protect customers' personal information. Banks and credit unions answer to their own regulators. For most other businesses that offer financial products or services to consumers, the FTC enforces it through the **Safeguards Rule**, 16 CFR Part 314. The FTC rewrote the rule in 2021, and most of it took effect June 9, 2023. A duty to report certain security events to the FTC took effect May 13, 2024.

"Financial institution" is broader than most owners expect. It includes tax preparers, mortgage brokers, appraisers, check cashers, money wirers, retailers with their own credit cards, and auto dealers that lease vehicles for more than 90 days. If you're not sure, see the FTC's business guide or ask an attorney.

**Customer information** is any paper or electronic record with nonpublic personal information about your customers that you or your service providers handle: names tied to Social Security numbers, account and loan numbers, income and tax figures, credit reports, driver's license images, and the fact that someone is your customer.

## The nine elements

The rule requires a written information security program that fits your size, your work, and how sensitive the information is. It must include:

1. **A Qualified Individual** who oversees and enforces the program, whether an employee or someone at an affiliate or service provider. You still own compliance and must designate a senior person to oversee any provider.
2. **A written risk assessment** of reasonably foreseeable risks to customer information, how well current safeguards control them, and how each risk will be reduced or accepted. Repeat it periodically.
3. **Safeguards** for those risks:
   - access controls and an inventory of the data, people, devices, systems, and facilities you rely on,
   - encryption in transit over external networks and at rest, or compensating controls the Qualified Individual approves in writing,
   - secure development practices and a way to evaluate software you buy,
   - multi-factor authentication for anyone accessing any information system, unless the Qualified Individual approves an equivalent control in writing,
   - secure disposal of customer information no later than two years after you last used it for that customer, unless you have a legitimate business or legal reason to keep it,
   - change management, and monitoring and logging of authorized users.
4. **Regular testing.** Continuous monitoring, or an annual penetration test plus vulnerability scans at least every six months and after material changes.
5. **Trained staff.** Security awareness training for everyone, updated as risks change, and qualified security staff whose skills stay current.
6. **Service provider oversight.** Choose providers that can protect the information, require safeguards in the contract, and reassess them periodically.
7. **A current program**, updated as testing, assessments, and business changes show what needs to change.
8. **A written incident response plan** covering roles and decision authority, communications, fixing weaknesses, documentation, and a review after each event.
9. **A written report to the board**, or to a senior officer if there's no board, at least once a year, on the program's status and material matters such as risk decisions, test results, and security events.

**Fewer than 5,000 consumers?** You don't need the written risk assessment, the penetration tests and scans, the written incident response plan, or the annual board report. Everything else still applies, including MFA, encryption, training, and service provider oversight. Most small tax practices pass 5,000 once former clients are counted, so count carefully.

## Reporting to the FTC

You must notify the FTC within 30 days of discovering a **notification event** involving the information of at least 500 consumers. That's unauthorized acquisition of unencrypted customer information. Encrypted data counts as unencrypted if the key was also taken, and unauthorized access is presumed to be acquisition unless you have reliable evidence it wasn't.

Report through the form on the FTC's website, which can make reports public. The clock starts when any employee, officer, or agent, other than the person who caused the event, knows about it. State breach notification laws apply separately.

**Tax preparers:** the IRS says the rule requires a written information security plan (WISP), and paid preparers agree to protect taxpayer data when they renew their PTIN each year. The IRS publishes a sample WISP (Publication 5708) and a security guide (Publication 4557).

## What every staff member needs to know

The rule sets no training frequency. Most firms train at hire and at least yearly, with short reminders during tax season or other peaks.

1. **Use MFA everywhere**, and never approve a sign-in prompt you didn't start.
2. **Spot phishing and pretexting.** Tax season brings fake "new client" emails with malicious attachments, fake IRS or e-file messages, and calls asking for client details.
3. **Verify payment and banking changes by phone**, using a number you already have, before changing where a refund, payout, or payment goes.
4. **Send customer information only through approved, encrypted channels**, such as the client portal. Not plain email, personal accounts, or unapproved AI tools.
5. **Look only at records your work needs**, on approved devices.
6. **Lock screens, offices, and file cabinets.** Shred paper and hand old devices to IT for wiping.
7. **Report anything unusual right away**: a lost laptop, a misdirected email, a client saying someone filed a return in their name, or a computer acting strangely. Know who the Qualified Individual is and how to reach them after hours.

## What the business needs on paper

- The written security program naming the Qualified Individual, the written risk assessment and each repeat, and a data and system inventory.
- Written approvals for encryption or MFA exceptions, and test and scan reports.
- Training records ([training log](../../templates/training-log.csv), [acknowledgment form](../../templates/acknowledgment-form.md)).
- Service provider contracts and review records.
- The retention and disposal policy, with disposal records.
- The incident response plan and incident records, including FTC reports, and each annual board report.

## Penalties and enforcement

The FTC enforces the rule through investigations and lawsuits against tax preparers, auto dealers, mortgage companies, and others. Most cases end with a consent order requiring a security program, independent assessments for years (often 20), and reporting to the FTC. Violating an order can bring civil penalties for each violation. State attorneys general can act too, and a breach brings its own costs in notification, credit monitoring, lost clients, and lawsuits.

The usual mistakes are not realizing the business is a "financial institution," a WISP filed but never used, MFA on email but not on tax software or the client portal, and former clients' files kept forever. Ask an attorney whether you're a financial institution, whether an event triggers an FTC report, and how to answer an FTC inquiry.

## Check yourself

1. Is a three-person tax preparation office covered by the Safeguards Rule?
2. A laptop holding 800 clients' returns is stolen. Its disk wasn't encrypted. What's the FTC deadline?
3. Your business keeps information on 3,000 consumers. Which requirements don't apply?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Yes. Tax preparation services are named as an example of a financial institution under the rule.
2. Notify the FTC as soon as possible and no later than 30 days after discovery, because it involves unencrypted information on at least 500 consumers. State laws may also require notices.
3. The written risk assessment, penetration testing and vulnerability scans, the written incident response plan, and the annual board report. Everything else still applies.
