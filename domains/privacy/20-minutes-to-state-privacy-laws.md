**Read this if** your business keeps personal information about customers, clients, or employees. That's nearly every business. It matters most if you have customers in many states, run a website or app, sell online, or handle IDs, account numbers, or health details.

## There's no single U.S. privacy law

A business answers to **sector laws** such as HIPAA, the FTC Safeguards Rule, FERPA, and COPPA (each has its own walkthrough here), **Section 5 of the FTC Act**, which bans unfair or deceptive practices such as breaking your own privacy promises, **state breach notification laws**, **state data security laws** such as New York's SHIELD Act, and **comprehensive state privacy laws**, which about two dozen states have passed.

State laws follow the person, not the business. A New York company with customers in California, Texas, and Virginia may owe duties under all four states' laws.

## Breach notification: every state

All 50 states, the District of Columbia, and U.S. territories require notice when certain personal information is breached. Details vary:

- **What counts.** Usually a name plus a Social Security number, driver's license or ID number, or a financial account or card number with its access code. Many states add biometrics, medical information, and online account credentials.
- **Encryption.** Most states don't require notice when the data was encrypted and the key wasn't taken.
- **Deadlines.** Some say "without unreasonable delay." A growing number set a fixed limit, often 30 to 60 days.
- **Who else to tell.** Many require notice to the state attorney general, and to credit reporting agencies above a set number of residents.
- **Vendors.** A service provider holding data for someone else usually must tell the data owner, which then notifies people.

Plan for the strictest state where you have customers, and get legal help when a breach happens.

## New York: the SHIELD Act

New York's Stop Hacks and Improve Electronic Data Security (SHIELD) Act applies to any business that owns or licenses computerized private information of New York residents, wherever the business is located.

**Breach notice (GBL § 899-aa).** "Private information" includes a name or identifier with an unencrypted Social Security number, driver's license or ID number, account or card number (with or without a code, if it can be used without one), biometric data, medical information, or health insurance information. A username or email address with its password or security answer counts on its own.

- Affected residents get notice in the most expedient time possible and **no later than 30 days after discovery**.
- The business also notifies the Attorney General, the Department of State, and the State Police, plus the Department of Financial Services if it's DFS-regulated, and the credit reporting agencies if more than 5,000 New Yorkers are notified at once.
- A business that notifies people under HIPAA, GLBA, or the DFS cybersecurity rule sends no second notice to individuals but still notifies the state agencies. HIPAA covered entities must notify the Attorney General within five business days of notifying HHS.

**Reasonable safeguards (GBL § 899-bb).** A business must keep reasonable administrative, technical, and physical safeguards for private information. Examples in the law include designating employees to run the program, identifying risks, **training and managing employees in the program's practices and procedures**, requiring safeguards from service providers by contract, and disposing of private information within a reasonable time.

A **small business** has fewer than 50 employees, less than $3 million in gross annual revenue in each of the last three fiscal years, or less than $5 million in year-end total assets. It complies if its safeguards are reasonable for its size and complexity, the nature of its activities, and the sensitivity of the information. Businesses already complying with HIPAA, GLBA, or the DFS cybersecurity rule are treated as compliant with the safeguards section.

The Attorney General enforces both parts. For notice failures, a court can award the greater of $5,000 or up to $20 per failed notice, capped at $250,000, for knowing or reckless violations. For safeguard failures, the Attorney General can seek civil penalties under the General Business Law.

## Comprehensive state privacy laws

California's Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA), as amended by the CPRA, came first. By late 2026, about two dozen states had passed comprehensive laws, including Colorado, Connecticut, Virginia, Texas, Oregon, and New Jersey. New York had not. They share a pattern.

**Who's covered.** Most apply only above a size threshold, commonly businesses that process data on 100,000 or more state residents a year, or fewer if they make money from selling data. California also covers businesses with annual gross revenue above $26,625,000 (the 2025 adjusted figure). Texas and a few others cover most businesses that aren't federally defined small businesses, and some duties, such as consent for selling sensitive data, apply even to small ones. Many exempt data covered by HIPAA, GLBA, or FERPA.

**Consumer rights.** Residents can ask to know, access, correct, and delete their data and get a portable copy. They can opt out of the sale of their data, targeted advertising, and certain profiling. Many states require opt-in consent before processing sensitive data such as health, precise location, biometrics, and data about children. Several states, including California and Colorado, require honoring browser opt-out signals such as Global Privacy Control.

**Business duties.** A clear privacy notice, collecting only what's reasonably necessary, reasonable security, contracts with processors (vendors) that limit how they use the data, and written data protection assessments for higher-risk processing such as targeted advertising, selling data, or processing sensitive data. California's regulations, updated January 1, 2026, add risk assessments, cybersecurity audits, and automated decision-making rules, phased in over several years. They also require everyone who handles privacy requests to know the law and how to direct consumers to exercise their rights.

**Enforcement.** State attorneys general enforce these laws, and California also has a dedicated agency, the California Privacy Protection Agency. California's administrative fines run up to $2,663 per violation and $7,988 per intentional violation or violation involving minors. California also lets consumers sue over certain data breaches caused by unreasonable security.

## What every staff member needs to know

1. **Collect only what you need**, don't keep it longer than needed, and keep it in approved systems, not personal email or unapproved apps, including AI tools.
2. **Use multi-factor authentication**, lock devices, and spot phishing. Stolen credentials start most breaches.
3. **Share personal data only with approved vendors** that have a contract.
4. **Route privacy requests** ("delete my data," "what do you have on me," "stop selling my data") to the designated person the same day. Deadlines run from when anyone receives them, commonly 45 days to respond.
5. **Don't promise what the privacy notice doesn't say.** Breaking a privacy promise is a deception case for the FTC.
6. **Report lost devices, misdirected emails, and suspected breaches right away.** New York's 30-day clock starts at discovery.

## What the business needs on paper

- A data inventory: what personal data you hold, where, why, and for how long.
- A privacy notice that matches what you actually do.
- A written security program, sized to the business (the SHIELD Act's list is a good outline), and an incident response plan with state-by-state notice steps.
- Training records. See the [training log](../../templates/training-log.csv).
- Vendor contracts with security and data use terms.
- A privacy request log and data protection assessments, where a comprehensive state law applies.

Common traps are a privacy notice copied from another website, ad and analytics tags that amount to "selling" or "sharing" data under state law with no opt-out, and ignoring Global Privacy Control signals where required.
